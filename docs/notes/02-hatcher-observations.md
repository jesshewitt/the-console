# Observations: Bradford Hatcher, *Yijing Word by Word* (2009)

Source: Volume 1 PDF, *The Book of Changes: Yijing, Word by Word*, Bradford Hatcher, 2009.
Accessed via: https://hermetica.info/Yijing1+2.pdf (mirrored at lightforcenetwork.com)
Pages read directly: Preface (pp.1-2), Notes on This Translation (pp.3-25), Methods of Divination (pp.45-49), Introductory Notes (pp.51-56).

All quotes below are from Hatcher's own text, extracted from the PDF.

---

## 1. What model of change does the I Ching use?

- Hatcher rejects the common summary that the Yijing is "founded upon a philosophy which describes Change as a product of the interaction of the two universal forces known as Yin and Yang." He lists this explicitly as one of eight claims that are false. Yin-Yang cosmology is a later overlay, not original to the Zhouyi.
- His model is situational, not cosmological. Each hexagram describes a landscape - a moment with its own contours and forks. Change is addressed through attitude adjustment. From his text: "when the situation is vastly bigger than we are, and it is presenting itself in a certain typical pattern, then what can be changed most handily is our response to it. In this response lies choice."
- Prognostications like "unfortunate" are not event predictions. They point at attitudes and their likely outcomes: "'unfortunate' means 'look at this wrong approach.'" The opposite attitude is always implied.

---

## 2. What does it offer the consulter?

- Access to a choice at one remove from the ego. Hatcher compares the Yijing to the psychiatrist who faces away from the patient: "With the Yijing, well, it's a book: like a door, it opens and closes... Its intent, and not-so-hidden motive, is only its original intent: to serve the reader, with assistance in finding the attitude which will optimize a situation, without hurting the reader's feelings."
- Fortune-telling is the entry point, not the destination. Directly: "Readers who come to the Yijing in search of a fortune teller, seeking objective answers to questions about their fate, soon learn that this function of the book is not its driving purpose, but merely its customary and initiatory means of access; it might even be called the bait."
- A prompt without explanation. He is explicit that his commentaries do not explain the hexagram texts. The tool's job is to stimulate the consulter's own thinking, not conclude it.

---

## 3. What makes it work as a divination tool?

- Vertical ambiguity: the text is deliberately constructed to speak to readers at different stages of understanding. The same passage works at a literal, psychological, philosophical, and humorous level. This is not vagueness; it is designed range.
- The response is a metaphor the consulter must apply. "The response, rather than the answer, will be a metaphor. It is up to the inquirer to make sense of this." Passive reception is not available; the tool requires active interpretation.
- The question frame shapes the response. Hatcher recommends writing the question down with a specified time span. An imprecise question gets a response that slides past the actual situation.
- No universal claims. "No statement is meant to be true or warranted for all times in all situations." It works like a language containing contradictory words (up and down) without advocating contradictory directions.

---

## 4. What is structurally universal vs. culturally/historically specific?

**Likely to translate:**
- The situational model: moments have characteristic shapes; what you can most readily change is your response. This is prior to any cosmological framework.
- The architecture of 64 situations from paired trigrams, each with six line-positions. Hatcher treats this structure as genuinely load-bearing, distinct from later overlays.
- The design principle of vertical ambiguity: a text that answers different users differently by being concrete and metaphorical at once.
- The insight that fortune-telling is bait for attitude adjustment and choice-framing.

**Requires translation or replacement:**
- The Ten Wings. Hatcher is direct: they import Yin-Yang cosmology and Confucian moral hierarchy that were not in the original Zhouyi. He reads them as often unreliable guides to the original intent.
- The Confucian moral vocabulary - junzi, hierarchy of merit, specific social roles. Hatcher strips these; a new system should do the same.
- The historical images (crossing rivers, sacrificing oxen). These were already metaphors when written; they can be replaced with new images carrying the same structural weight.
- The divination ritual (yarrow stalks, coins). Hatcher presents these as culturally contingent access methods, not essential to the book's function.
