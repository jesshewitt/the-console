# Observations: Stephen Karcher, *Total I Ching: Myths for Change* (2003) and related work

**Source depth: paraphrased from secondary sources.**

## 1. What model of change does the I Ching use?

- Karcher's model is built around two older Chinese concepts that predate the Yin-Yang schema: *Da* (Great - concentrating, commanding force) and *Xiao* (Small - adapting, yielding force). He treats the alternation between these as more fundamental than the familiar yin-yang duality.
- Change in this frame is described as "a constant stepping back and forth between the opposites, always in motion, never putting both feet in the same place" (paraphrased from his essay). Movement is not directional progress but perpetual oscillation.
- The agents of change are *shen* - bright spirits or inspiring forces - and *gui* - ghost-like forces that freeze and traumatize. These are not decorative mythology; Karcher treats them as the actual mechanism. The shen are, in his account, the "managers" of shifts in the larger field that play out in our lives and bodies. This directly inverts Wilhelm, where the agents are ethical qualities and social-moral forces.
- Critically, Karcher argues that historical layers of commentary progressively stripped the spirit-and-ancestor framework from the text. Wilhelm's philosophical-ethical tone reflects that stripping. Karcher reverses the direction, reading back toward the older, shamanic layer.

## 2. What does it offer the consulter?

- In his own words: "Its job is to make us deeply aware of the now, the present moment, and what is truly at play in the everyday events of our lives."
- Also: "Change puts you on the Way, vitalizes your imagining with its symbols, opens your heart - and that is enough."
- The goal is transformation, not prediction. Karcher frames the I Ching as a tool for "protection, insight, and self-realization" that works by accumulating inner force through the alternation of great and small practices. This is closer to a spiritual discipline than to a decision support tool.
- The consulter receives images drawn from Bronze Age mythology - stories, figures, ancestral situations - that Karcher treats as alive rather than historical. The function is not orientation within a cycle (as in Wilhelm) or attitude adjustment (as in Hatcher) but contact with a living inner world that the symbols represent.
- Secondary sources describe Karcher as reading each hexagram in present tense as a living myth, not a static commentary. The text speaks as if things are happening now.

---

## 3. What makes it work as a divination tool?

- The symbols carry multiple layers simultaneously. By recovering the oldest layer - Bronze Age imagery of spirits, ancestors, and ritual - Karcher argues the hexagrams become richer rather than narrower. Each image has more threads to pull.
- The "angry ghost" motif is illustrative. Hexagram 18, in Karcher's reading, concerns "a curse laid by the ghost of a dead ancestor" rather than Wilhelm's more abstract "work on what has been spoiled." The concrete spirit-presence gives the consulter something visceral to work with. (The biroco.com reviewer notes Karcher applies this motif more broadly than strictly warranted - which is itself informative about his method: he leans into the mythic layer even when it becomes formulaic.)
- His character-by-character translation (in the earlier Ritsema/Karcher Eranos edition) exposes the associative chain of each Chinese character. This lets a reader trace symbolic themes across hexagrams that stay hidden in smoother translations. The tool becomes more productive the more you know its vocabulary.
- Unlike Wilhelm's approach, which works largely through narrative commentary, Karcher's approach works through image and mythic resonance. You do not reason your way to an interpretation - you find yourself inside a story.

---

## 4. What is structurally universal vs. culturally/historically specific?

**Karcher's implicit position** (drawn from how he frames the project):

- He treats the underlying psychological patterns as universal - the need for contact with inspiring forces, the danger of being frozen by what is unresolved, the alternation of great and small - while the Chinese mythological forms are one culturally specific expression of those patterns.
- He explicitly connects the I Ching to what he calls Jungian concepts (secondary sources describe his work as Jungian; he uses "archetypal" language - his term, not my framing). The Bronze Age imagery is not Chinese-specific in his view; it accesses something that myths across cultures are pointing at.
- The spirit/ghost framework (*shen*/*gui*) is presented as historically specific to ancient China but pointing at psychologically universal experiences: inspiration, trauma, the pull of unfinished family patterns.

**What this implies for a new system:**

- The layered-image approach is transferable: the value is in giving the consulter something mythically resonant and concrete, not in the specific Chinese figures used. New figures from different traditions could carry the same function.
- The present-tense, living-story approach to hexagram text is formally separable from its Chinese content.
- The *shen*/*gui* polarity maps loosely to what other traditions call inspiration/obstruction - a real structural axis, even if the specific cosmology is Chinese.
- What would not transfer: the specific Bronze Age Chinese ritual context, the ancestor-consultation frame, and the original divinatory occasion (royal decisions about war and sacrifice). These are interesting historically but are not load-bearing in a new design.
