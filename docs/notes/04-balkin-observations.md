# Observations: Jack Balkin, *The Laws of Change: I Ching and the Philosophy of Life* (2002, rev. 2009)

## 1. What model of change does the I Ching use?

- Balkin's title is his thesis: the I Ching describes "laws of change" - patterns of how situations develop, reverse, and cycle that are stable enough to learn from. Change is not random and not fully controlled by human intention; it has regularities that a person can study and align with.
- His core claim, as described on his Yale page, is that "the I Ching is nothing less than an explanation of the laws of change in the world and how human beings can learn to live in harmony with them." The word "harmony" is load-bearing here: the goal is not mastery or prediction but attunement.
- Unlike Karcher, who grounds change in spirit-forces and mythic alternation, Balkin's model is philosophical and ethical. The forces operating in the world are things like social dynamics, the natural rhythm of accumulation and release, the tendency of extremes to reverse. These are observable patterns, not cosmological entities.
- Balkin works from the Wilhelm-Baynes translation (standard for English-language philosophical commentary) and treats the Confucian ethical overlay as a feature, not a distortion. The I Ching in his reading is already a philosophical text, not something that needs archaeology to recover a truer layer.

---

## 2. What does it offer the consulter?

- Balkin frames the book as "practical philosophy and ethics" aimed at helping people "cultivate their characters, achieve emotional balance, and maintain personal integrity in the face of adversity and ever-changing circumstances." The consultation is in service of character formation, not situation management.
- The secondary sources describe him as offering detailed ethical and philosophical commentary on each hexagram - less about predicting what will happen and more about what posture, value, or quality of attention is called for in a situation of this type.
- The biroco.com review quotes one of his commentaries directly, giving a sense of the voice: when addressing a line about empty ritual, Balkin advises the reader to "come to terms with this immediately and decide what it is you really want." The advice is psychological and practical, not cosmological.
- He is explicit that the I Ching does not tell you what to do. It functions as a prompt and a frame. The decision remains with the consulter.

---

## 3. What makes it work as a divination tool?

- Balkin's account of the mechanism is secular and cognitive. He does not invoke synchronicity or any claim that the casting method picks a meaningful hexagram. Instead, he argues that the I Ching's "vague and cryptic" language does the work: it "induces us to abandon logical and linear thinking" and stimulates intuition and lateral thinking.
- The Goodreads description adds a useful gloss: Balkin sees consulting the I Ching as appropriate specifically "when you don't have a clear rational way to proceed" - at that point, the oracle offers "out of the box thinking" rather than a better application of the same reasoning that has already stalled.
- In this framing, the divination mechanism is not special. Any sufficiently open-ended prompt would do similar work. What the I Ching adds is a philosophical framework around that prompt - 64 carefully worked situations that have accumulated centuries of ethical commentary. The randomness breaks the rut; the text supplies the alternative frame.
- This is a notably modest and non-mysterious account. It sidesteps the question of why casting coins produces a useful hexagram by saying the usefulness comes from what you do with the text afterward, not from the casting.

---

## 4. What is structurally universal vs. culturally/historically specific?

- Balkin's position appears to be that the I Ching's philosophical core is broadly human - the dynamics it describes (situations of conflict, of waiting, of collaboration, of reversals, of integrity under pressure) are not culturally bounded. Secondary sources emphasize that he presents the text as applicable to "everyday problems" without requiring the reader to understand its Chinese cultural context.
- At the same time, he does not strip or erase the cultural material. His book includes a history of the I Ching's development and a survey of consulting methods. The cultural specificity is treated as context worth knowing, not as an obstacle to be translated away.
- His ethical commentary draws on the Confucian interpretive tradition - the Ten Wings - which is itself a historical and culturally specific layer. Balkin treats this layer as philosophically serious rather than contingent, which aligns him with Wilhelm and distinguishes him from Karcher (who reaches behind the Confucian layer to the older shamanic layer) and Hatcher (who tries to get under all interpretive layers to the lexical base).
- The unstated assumption in his approach is that the ethical situations the I Ching describes are universal because human character and social life have enough constants that Bronze Age observations about power, patience, and integrity remain applicable. This is a philosophical wager rather than an argument - he does not defend it extensively, at least as described by secondary sources.
